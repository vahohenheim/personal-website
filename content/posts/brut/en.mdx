<Section n="01" label="context" title="One truck, five addresses">

BRUT sells fried bites, crispy outside and melting inside, three recipes and a single sauce, from a truck that parks in a different spot around Tours every week. A customer’s first question is not “what is it?”, it is “where are you this week?”.

The brief came down to four verbs: tell people where to find the truck, make them hungry, collect sign-ups to announce the dates, collect reviews. One constraint shaped everything: I was leaving to spend a year abroad. The founder had to be able to change a date, a price or a photo without me, and without breaking anything.

</Section>

<Section n="02" label="product" title="What the visitor sees">

A home page that reads from top to bottom like a visit to the truck: a video, the ritual (grab, dip, bite), the three recipes, the menu, the locations, the newsletter, the contact. Three versions of this page were built side by side so the client could choose; the third one won.

</Section>

### Locations, without an interactive map

The first version used a Leaflet map. It was replaced by an illustrated map of the Tours area, a plain image, with numbered dots: lighter, better looking, and easier to read for five points. Each dot opens a bubble with the period, the place and the opening hours.

Dates are entered with a start and an end: past locations disappear on their own, without anyone having to remove them. And “Get there” does not choose for the visitor: it offers Google Maps, Apple Maps, or copying the address.

<Section n="03" label="architecture" title="The architecture">

A showcase site, but one whose entire content lives in a database. The equation to solve: pages as fast as a static site, and editable like a CMS, without paying for a CMS.

</Section>

<Diagram
  src="/posts/brut/schema-architecture"
  width={1040}
  height={600}
  alt="Architecture of the BRUT site: the visitor gets pre-rendered pages from the content cache, with no database query.   caption="The visitor never touches the database: they get pre-rendered pages. Only forms write, and only a publish renews the cache."
/>

<Section>

The centrepiece is a single read function, marked `"use cache"` and tagged `site-content` thanks to the Cache Components of Next.js 16. Public pages are pre-rendered with its result and served from the CDN. The admin’s “Publish” button invalidates that tag: the next visit reads the database again, then everyone gets the new version. The sitemap and the structured data follow the same cycle.

If the database cannot be reached, the site shows the default values written in the code and tries again a few minutes later. It never goes down because Neon has a hiccup.

</Section>

<Section n="04" label="admin" title="An admin built for the truck">

The admin is half of the project, and the half the founder uses every week. It comes down to two screens: a dashboard to see how things are going, and content editing to change what visitors see. It uses the site’s visual identity, so that he feels at home rather than in a back office.

### The dashboard: everything at a glance

At the top, four figures with their thirty-day trend: newsletter sign-ups, messages received, customer reviews, average rating. Below, the activity day by day, the three forms stacked; clicking a figure isolates its curve. All the calculations are done by the database, one query per block: nothing is counted again in the browser.

</Section>

### Editing the content: one schema, forms

Every text, price, date and photo path is described once, in a schema. That schema generates the seventeen sections of the form, validates the input on the server and types the content the components read. Each field carries help written for the founder, not for a developer: “The location disappears from the site the next day.” Adding a field is one line of schema and a default value; the form follows.

</Section>

<Section>

<PlusList>
  <Plus title="Saving is not publishing.">
    The founder prepares several sections, reviews them, and only puts them online when he wants to.
    A line at the top of the screen always says whether the site shows the latest version.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="Bold, and nothing else.">
    Taglines such as “C’est bon parce que c’est vrai.” go through a Tiptap editor limited to bold,
    stored as text, never as HTML. There is no way to break the layout.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="Protected twice.">
    A password at the door, then a check in every page and every action: never rely on the front
    door alone. Pages kept out of search engines, never cached, no subscriber data in the logs.
  </Plus>
</PlusList>

</Section>

<Section n="05" label="forms" title="Sign-ups, reviews, messages">

Three forms, a single path. Every submission goes through Vercel BotID, an invisible honeypot field and a minimum filling time, then is written to Neon before anything else. Sign-ups are then copied to Brevo, after the visitor has their answer; unsubscriptions come back through a webhook, and a nightly job resyncs everything. There is only one list of subscribers, wherever people sign up.

Reviews come in through a QR code stuck on the truck. The page it opens is neither in the navigation nor in search engines: two minutes, a rating, the recipes tasted. The button to Google reviews is shown to everyone, whatever the rating: Google requires it, and it is more honest.

</Section>

<Section n="06" label="finishing" title="The details that matter">

<PlusList>
  <Plus title="A video that really starts.">
    Two encodings (HEVC for Retina screens, H.264 as a fallback), a version turned a quarter turn
    for phones in portrait, a blurred poster image. And an iOS bug tracked down: a video element
    without a source in the server render made the native play button appear.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="Photos prepared by hand.">
    1,600 px wide for dense screens, sRGB profile, mozjpeg compression: a 254 MB source video became
    about fifteen megabytes.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="Functions in Frankfurt,">
    next to the database, rather than in the United States by default.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="Two audits before calling it “done”.">
    Performance and security first (a critical vulnerability fixed by a Next.js update, security
    headers), then a regulatory audit: a complete privacy policy, a consumer mediator, the service
    providers named.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="Local SEO.">
    Structured data for a food establishment and its menu, generated from the content, and a sitemap
    dated from the last publish.
  </Plus>
</PlusList>

<Section n="07" label="performance" title="Measure, then lighten">

A food truck’s site is read standing up, on a phone, with the kind of 4G you get in a car park. So I measured it in bad conditions: Lighthouse, an emulated mid-range phone, simulated slow 4G, three runs per page in production, and the median. Then I lightened what was heavy, and measured again.

### Five changes, nothing visible

<PlusList>
  <Plus title="Images in AVIF,">
    with WebP as a fallback: the photo of La Bête goes from 210 to 83 KB.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="A single poster image for the video,">
    in a `picture` element that picks landscape or portrait, instead of two preloaded images.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="Logos at the right size.">
    Without a `sizes` attribute, they were sent 1,920 px wide for 72 to 360 px displayed.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="The video after the page.">
    It is only added once the page has loaded, and never in data saver mode, on 2G, or when the
    visitor asks for reduced motion.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="The italic font is no longer preloaded:">
    the browser only downloads what is displayed.
  </Plus>
</PlusList>

### What I tried, then dropped

<PlusList>
  <Plus title="CSS inlined in the HTML.">
    One network round trip saved, but the CSS was copied into the RSC payload: the compressed HTML
    of the home page went from 17 to 65 KB.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="A re-encoded video.">
    10 MB became 4, at the cost of visible blocks on the frying bubbles. A re-encode is done from
    the 4K original, not from the published file.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="A lighter font.">
    Removing the optical size axis of Newsreader halved the font, but changed the drawing of the
    large titles. The typographic choice won.
  </Plus>
</PlusList>

<Callout label="method">
  Lighthouse 13.5, three runs per page in production, median kept. Mobile: emulated Moto G Power,
  simulated slow 4G, CPU slowed down four times. No layout shift (CLS 0), a server that answers in 5
  to 16 ms thanks to pre-rendered pages, accessibility from 96 to 100, best practices and SEO at 100
  on the indexed pages.
</Callout>

<Section n="08" label="handover" title="Handing over">

For what goes beyond the admin, I prepared a handover to someone who does not code. A file of instructions for a coding assistant: speak without jargon, announce before acting, one question at a time, always suggest the admin before touching the code, and never put anything online without an explicit “yes”. All it takes is to open the folder and write “put it online” or “change the price of the box of 5”.

Behind it, the safety net: documentation with one page per topic, a log where each request fits on one line, and 137 tests (unit tests, then journeys on desktop and phone) run on every push, plus smoke tests on the live site after each production release.

<Section n="09" label="takeaways" title="What I take away">

<PlusList>
  <Plus title="Show rather than describe.">
    Three versions online side by side settled in a day what a meeting would not have settled.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="The simplest often wins.">
    An image instead of an interactive map, bold instead of a rich text editor.
  </Plus>
  <Plus title="The client’s feedback is the roadmap.">
    The log holds dozens of them, each recorded on one line with the files it touched.

  <Plus title="Documentation is part of the deliverable.">
